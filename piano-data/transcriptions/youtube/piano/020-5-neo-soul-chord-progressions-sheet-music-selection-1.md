What is Modal Interchange?
Modal interchange is a technique that involves borrowing chords from a parallel mode or scale to use within a key. In short, while playing in a major key, you can temporarily borrow chords built on the same tonic from its parallel minor mode, or vice versa. For example, if you are playing in F major, you might introduce a chord borrowed from F minor—such as D flat major—to add fresh color without actually modulating. Borrowing can also happen by drawing from other modes, such as Dorian or Lydian. Typically, composers only take one or two chords at a time before returning to the original home key, keeping the tonal center intact.

Note on altered dominants: the "AD" label is replaced by "MI" when they are part of a 2-5 progression borrowed directly from the parallel mode leading to the target chord, making them fully diatonic to that source mode. For example, in progression No. 1, the sequence Aø(b9) - D7(b9) - Gmaj9 features a D7(b9) chord that is not altered within the G minor mode (even though it would be relative to G Major). In these cases, the "MI" label is attributed to the chord instead of "AD".

In the sheet music for these progressions, the analytical tools are indicated with specific abbreviations and colored symbols:
MI: Modal Interchange
AD: Altered Dominants
SD: Secondary Dominants (sometimes with the relative second degree, labeled S2)
TS: Tritone Substitution
DC: Deceptive Cadence

Please note that for DC, for readability reasons, the symbol applies only to the final chord—the one that "substitutes" the expected target chord—although a "Deceptive Cadence" inherently refers to the entire sequence in question.
